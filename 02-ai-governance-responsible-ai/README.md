# 🛡️ AI Governance & Responsible AI

### Governance designed to enable responsible speed

Enterprise AI governance should not be a committee that appears at the end of delivery. It should create **clear decision rights, proportional controls, reusable standards, and fast escalation paths** throughout the AI lifecycle.

> **Portfolio note:** This generalized framework is designed for enterprise and regulated environments and does not disclose confidential employer policies or controls.

---

## Governance Principles

- **Risk-based, not one-size-fits-all:** controls scale with materiality, data sensitivity, autonomy, and business impact.
- **Govern early:** identify security, privacy, legal, compliance, data, and Responsible AI requirements before build decisions harden.
- **Keep humans accountable:** AI can support decisions; accountable business and technology owners remain explicit.
- **Separate guidance from material decisions:** routine questions move quickly while sensitive issues follow defined escalation paths.
- **Design for production:** ownership, monitoring, support, access, evaluation, and change control are part of readiness.

---

## Governance Architecture

| Layer | Purpose | Typical Participants |
|---|---|---|
| **AI Front Door** | Early guidance, ideas, patterns and routing | AI enablement, business teams, builders |
| **Structured Intake** | Capture outcome, users, data, integrations, actions and ownership | Business owner + delivery team |
| **Risk & Readiness Review** | Determine proportional controls and required reviewers | Security, Privacy, Legal, Compliance, Data, Architecture |
| **AI Leadership Decision** | Resolve material risk, policy, investment or enterprise alignment decisions | Executive AI leadership / steering body |
| **Production Readiness** | Confirm controls, ownership, evaluation, monitoring and support | Business + Technology + Risk partners |
| **Ongoing Oversight** | Monitor performance, incidents, adoption, value and material change | Product/business owner + governance partners |

## Lightweight Go / No-Go Questions

Before an AI capability advances, leaders should be able to answer:

1. What **business outcome** are we trying to improve, and how will value be measured?
2. Who are the **users and accountable business owner**?
3. Is this best solved with an existing AI capability, a configurable agent, or custom engineering?
4. What **data** will the solution access, and how sensitive is it?
5. What **connectors, APIs, or enterprise systems** are required?
6. Can the AI **take actions**, or does it only retrieve, summarize, recommend, or draft?
7. Where is **human review or approval** required?
8. Who owns the capability after launch, including monitoring and support?
9. What would prevent this solution from being **production ready**?
10. Does any issue require **executive escalation, risk acceptance, or policy interpretation**?

---

## Decision Rights

Routine guidance should remain accessible and fast. Material decisions move to the appropriate private leadership forum when they involve significant data exposure, external/customer impact, regulated decisions, autonomous actions, policy exceptions, material financial/reputational risk, or enterprise-wide standards.

This creates a governance model that is both **accessible at the edge and accountable at the center**.

---

## Production Readiness

A production AI capability should have clear ownership, approved data access, security and privacy controls, defined human oversight, evaluation criteria, monitoring, incident/escalation procedures, change control, user enablement, and measurable business outcomes.

The objective is not merely to approve an AI solution. It is to ensure the enterprise can **operate it responsibly after launch**.

---

## Executive Outcome

Effective governance reduces uncertainty and rework while giving executives confidence that AI can scale without losing control of risk, accountability, or value.
